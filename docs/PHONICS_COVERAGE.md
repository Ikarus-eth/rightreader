# Phonics coverage — Moonflower r8

The sound-picture guide covers all 44 phonemes in the traditional British-English inventory: 24 consonants and 20 vowels. Every phoneme has one unique anchor word and one unique icon. The book itself contains 43 of these sounds; only the traditional /ʊə/ vowel in cure is absent. The guide adds cure so the full inventory remains available.

All 265 distinct words in the story and chapter titles have authored sound breakdowns. Including picture anchors and practice examples, all 313 available words have breakdowns and George recordings. This is coverage of the sound inventory and this book’s spellings, not every possible English spelling or a complete teaching curriculum.

## Reading the breakdowns

- Silent letters add no phoneme. For example, would contains /w ʊ d/; its l is silent.
- Split vowel spellings stay in printed order, with linked tiles. Stone has five letter tiles but four sounds; o and e are highlighted together.
- One written group can represent two phonemes. Fox has four sounds; tapping x shows cat (/k/) then sun (/s/).
- Through is th | r | ough, representing /θ r uː/. There are no separate g or h sounds.
- Artus uses ar | t | u | s, /ɑː t ə s/, targeting the current English recording as requested.
- The short, unstressed ending in words such as tiny is grouped with the tree vowel; the cue explains its shorter realization.
- The traditional cure vowel is accent-dependent. Some British speakers merge it with the paw vowel. Other legitimate variants, including our, again, with and path, are noted in the word help.
- Anchor buttons play the whole example word. Isolated phoneme recordings are not provided.

## Sources and review limits

The inventory follows the traditional British-English pronunciation-symbol convention described by [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/). [Oxford’s cure entry](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/cure_2) gives /kjʊə(r)/; the app explicitly notes accent variation. The [Department for Education reading framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-reading-framework-teaching-the-foundations-of-literacy) explains grapheme–phoneme correspondences and split digraphs.

Mappings are authored data, checked for full spelling reconstruction, valid links, phoneme counts, all-word coverage and anchor uniqueness. Automated checks cannot certify every audible pronunciation. A parent/phonics-specialist listening review, especially proper names and accent variants, remains useful.
